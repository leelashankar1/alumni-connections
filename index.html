What we found

Being in the same room is not connection.

What seven alumni, 100 ideas, and two prototypes taught our team about designing for students and alumni at The Claremont Colleges.

Problem

Alumni engagement surveys from 2022 to 2024 put meaningful alumni engagement at about 19 to 20 percent (cited via US News in the project brief). Students feel distant from alumni, and existing platforms feel transactional.

My role

I took part in every stage with my team. I brainstormed about 30 of our 100 ideas, conducted 2 of 7 interviews, helped create the 5C Family Network prototype, gathered feedback on it from 4 people, helped build the revised flyer, and edited our video.

What we made

Two prototypes: the 5C Alumni Family Network (a program, tested with a flyer) and a Color War-style tournament for students and alumni.

Biggest lesson

Putting students and alumni in the same space does not create connection by itself. The connection has to be set up with structure, clear roles, and a way for everyone to contribute.

The brief

Alumni who want to help, students who do not know how to ask.

The Claremont Colleges (the 5Cs) are five undergraduate colleges on a shared campus in Claremont, California. The brief described alumni who are excited to help but do not know where to start, and students who feel distant from alumni because existing connection platforms do not feel human. Asking for career advice can feel transactional, especially when students worry about asking for help at all.

Two scoping decisions came first. We chose to connect students with alumni, not alumni with other alumni. And we chose to avoid the career-focused style that platforms like Sagehen Connect often offer.

  • EvidenceBrief cites 2022 to 2024 engagement surveys: about 19 to 20 percent of alumni engage meaningfully.
  • DecisionStudents with alumni, not alumni with alumni. Non-career by design.
  • My partPart of the four-person scoping conversation in week one.
  • If shippedAny solution has to work for the 80 percent who go silent, not the 20 percent already engaged.

What we heard

  • Friends bring people back. Alumni from Pomona '25 and Scripps '24 said the friendships formed in college keep them connected.
  • Graduation feels like losing a safety net. Alumni from Pomona '26 and Pomona '24 described going from a high-support campus to feeling alone.
  • Distance changes everything. Alumni still in Claremont find it much easier to stay connected than alumni in Bali or Berlin.
  • Casual beats formal. First interactions should feel casual and human, not transactional.
  • Lost traditions matter. Alumni from Pomona '95 and Pomona '83 both talked about traditions that no longer exist.
  • Alumni worry their advice is outdated.

Ideate

100 ideas, 7 categories, 2 picks.

We started with a "benefit baiting" exercise: list the benefits a product connecting students and alumni could offer, then think of existing products that share those benefits. Connection led us to families, video calls, and shared interests. From there we built a list of 100 ideas and sorted every one into a category. Each category answers one of our seven How Might We questions.

    Each tile is one idea. Hover or tap for the text. The ringed tile is idea 17, which became the Family Network. Ideas 16, 19, 56, and 98 are the game and competition ideas the Color War drew on.

        How we chose

        We did not score the list. We ran mini role-play tests of our strongest ideas with other groups in the class, acting out how each one would work, and picked the two that we liked the most and that our classmates responded to best in that activity. We decided which ideas were the strongest and prototyped those.

        What the two picks had in common, looking back: the Family Network (idea 17) served all four personas, especially the first two, so it was the idea with the widest reach. The Color War tournament came from our biggest group of ideas, the fun-together games, so it was the idea that tested our most common instinct. Both could be put in front of real people within a week, one with a flyer and one with a staged event, and neither leads with careers.

        • Evidence100 ideas, categorized. 7 HMW questions. The largest category was games and competitions (22).
        • DecisionRole-play the strongest ideas with classmates, then prototype the two that held up. A program, not an app.
        • My partAbout 30 of the 100 ideas.
        • If shippedThe other 98 ideas are a backlog. Traditions (9) and alumni voice (10) are untested and cheap to try.
        Prototype

        Two low-resolution prototypes.

        Prototype 1: 5C Alumni Family Network

        A program where students and alumni are placed in a "family" of 4 to 6 people and stay in it after graduation. We made a trifold flyer to explain it and used the flyer to collect feedback. It is a prototype for testing, not a final product.

        How joining works

        OrientationStudents opt in and take a short matching test.
        MatchingShared non-career interests, hobbies, personality traits, hometown.
        Your familyOne underclassman, one upperclassman, one recent alum, one older alum.
        First meetingThe program sets up the first meeting; families then meet on their own.
        GraduationYou stay in your family and move from receiving advice to giving it.
        Over timeSubsidized meals and activities; a way to switch families if it is not working.

        Program rules

        • Opt-in, at orientation.
        • Families of 4 to 6, spanning four stages of college life.
        • Matched on non-career interests, hobbies, personality, and hometown.
        • Careers and colleges are mixed on purpose, to keep it a non-networking space and strengthen ties between the five colleges.
        • Subsidized shared meals and activities.
        • A platform for sharing updates and creations.

        The flyer, revised version

        Low-resolution prototype for concept testing, built as a trifold in Canva. I helped create the first version and the revised version.

        Prototype 2: Color War tournament

        An event where seniors, 10-year alumni, and current MBA students play classic college games on mixed teams. We staged it with about 10 participants to see what mixing people in the same space actually does.

        What we were testing

        Whether shared, informal fun is enough on its own to make students and alumni connect. This was the largest category in our 100 ideas, so it was the hypothesis most worth checking.

        • EvidenceTwo prototypes people could react to: a flyer in hand and a staged event.
        • DecisionTest the join path and the rules, not the visual design. The flyer is deliberately rough.
        • My partHelped create both versions of the flyer.
        • If shippedThe join path (opt-in, test, match, first meeting) is the part to build first and measure.
        Test and iterate

        What ten people told us, and what we changed.

        Family Network flyer: 10 students and alumni gave feedback

        I gathered feedback from 4 of the 10, then used it to help build the revised flyer. Open a row to see the change.

        • Most people thought joining should be opt-in. We had planned to sign all first-years up automatically, with an option to opt out later.I collected 4 of the 10
          What we changed: the program became opt-in. This was the change that mattered most; see the reflection below.
        • Some pictures felt impersonal and did not show what the program is.
          What we changed: replaced the lawn and food photos with photos of alumni and families at campus events.
        • The program needed more clarity in places.
          What we changed: rewrote the "I'm in a family. What now?" section to explain that the program helps set up first meetings and then encourages families to meet on their own. Added how to switch families.
        • The flyer needed proofreading.
          What we changed: fixed the text in the revised version.
        • The name can make it sound like something for parents or families of students.
          What we decided: kept "5C Alumni Family Networks" for this round, since it is an early concept for testing and not a final product. Renaming is the first thing to do if it goes further.
        • Positive reactions from both students and alumni, who were interested in joining if it ever launches.
          One response: "I would totally join."

        Color War tournament: about 10 people took part

        • What worked. Participants liked that it felt fun, informal, and "very college." Many liked meeting people from other class years who shared common experiences.
        • What did not. With little structure, some groups took over the activities while others felt sidelined or unsure how to join. Students and alumni also drifted back to their own groups.
        • What we would change. More structured teams, rotations, and activities designed to mix people on purpose.

        We did not run a survey. With more time, the first thing I would add is a five-question survey to the ten flyer testers to size the opt-in preference rather than infer it from conversations.

        The video

        We told our user's story in a 2-minute video built from a "storyspine," focusing on the older alum persona with elements of the recent grad persona, to show how the Family Network could connect two people like them. I edited the video. It is drawn, not filmed, so no interviewee appears in it.

        • Evidence10 flyer testers, 6 recorded themes, 5 changes. About 10 Color War participants, observed.
        • DecisionReverse the enrollment model. Keep the name for now and flag it. Add structure to the event.
        • My part4 of the 10 flyer conversations; helped build v2; edited the video above.
        • If shippedMeasure engagement per network, not only networks created, so an opt-in program is judged on quality.
        If this launched

        How we would know it worked.

        A proposal, not a result. Nothing here has been measured.

        The system has three kinds of users: the alumni office manager who runs it, alumni, and current students. These are the measures I would set up for the office in the first year.

        • Family networks created per quarterThe count the office would report, and the number an opt-everyone policy would inflate. On its own it says nothing about quality.
        • Engagement rate per networkInteractions per family, quarterly and annually. Starting goal: at least two interactions per family per quarter. This is the measure that catches networks that exist on paper only.
        • Members per networkThe prototype requires a minimum of 4. The right minimum and maximum for engagement can be A/B tested; too many members may make a family less personal.
        • Who is in the engaged networksTrack member attributes the office already holds (gender, age, location, student year, major, job title). Once there is enough data, compare high-engagement and low-engagement networks to see what they have in common.
        What I took away

        We wanted a big network. We could not force people into it.

        A big lesson for me came in the iteration step. When we first designed the 5C Family Network, we planned to sign all first-years up automatically at orientation, and they could opt out later if they did not like it. We thought this would build a strong foundation, since most of each graduating class would already be in the network. When we tested the idea, most people thought it should be opt-in. I realized we had to prioritize the user and not put our goals for the product above them. We want a large network of 5C "families," but we cannot force people to be part of it.

        This connects to what we talked about in class: it is hard to keep your own wishes and needs out of the design process, and it was especially hard here because we were designing for ourselves in a way. Finding a balance between what is best for your user and your own idea of what the product should be is important.

        The rule I take from it is to balance the needs of the users with those of the alumni office. My hypothesis: opting everyone in would raise the office's count of family networks created, but most of those networks would have low or zero engagement. Alumni and students who are motivated enough to choose to join will form fewer networks, and better ones.

        Methods

        What each method means.

        Empathy interview
        A long, open conversation that digs for stories and feelings rather than opinions.
        Empathy map
        Four quadrants (say, do, think, feel) to separate what we observed from what we inferred.
        Point of view
        A one-line persona: a user, what they need, and the insight behind it.
        How might we
        A question that turns an insight into a prompt for ideas.
        Benefit baiting
        List the benefits a solution should give, name existing things that give them, and borrow from those.
        100 ideas
        Deliberate over-generation, wild to realistic, before choosing.
        Low-resolution prototype
        Something cheap and fast (a flyer, a staged event) built to test a concept, not to ship.
        Concept test
        Asking people whether they would want something, as opposed to whether they can use it.
        Storyspine
        A story scaffold ("once upon a time, every day, until one day") used to script the video.
        Journey map
        One persona's experience over time: stages, actions, feelings, and the gap worth designing for.
